Questions

Why Qloo, not genre tags or public similar-artist data?

Counterset needs every pair on your bill ranked, including small acts and acts that are not musicians. Qloo ranks exactly the acts you send it in one response per kind of act. On the Lollapalooza Saturday bill that covered 496 pairs both ways; ListenBrainz's public top-100 lists linked 11. Genre tags pair acts by label, not by audience, and found a mostly different set. Where ListenBrainz did link a pair, three of its four reciprocal pairs were also Qloo cues.

Does it change who plays?

No. The bill is fixed. Counterset only moves start times and stages, inside each act's availability, changeovers, stage hours, curfews and locks. Sets that have already started are never moved.

What does the AI actually do?

A model reads production messages and proposes typed constraints, each with the exact words it came from. You tick which ones to apply. A second model can brief you on the computed options; any number it uses that is not in the computed facts gets the briefing replaced by a plain summary. Models cannot call Qloo, solve, approve or publish.

What if an act is not in Qloo?

It stays unresolved. Every pair with that act is reported as unknown, never as zero, and you choose whether unknown pairs count. On Lollapalooza Saturday, 10 of 42 acts stayed unresolved rather than being matched by guesswork.

What happens when nothing fits?

The solver says so and lists the rules that cannot all hold, reduced until removing any one makes the rest satisfiable. It never shortens a set, relaxes a curfew or breaks a lock to make a schedule appear.

Is it proven to improve a festival?

Not yet. The software is tested and the evidence is inspectable, but no festival has used it and attendance effects are unmeasured. The next test is a programmer reviewing proposed moves, and attendees' own must-see pairs, which already take priority when you enter them.
